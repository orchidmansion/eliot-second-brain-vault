The old organic search measurement model, built largely around rankings, clicks, and sessions, is becoming less sufficient on its own in an AI search environment. And the more I talk to SEOs, marketers, and leadership teams, the clearer it becomes that most of us are still trying to adapt the old model rather than expand it into something fit for AI search.

Traditional search generally gave us a ranked list of links, more query-level positional visibility, a largely click-led journey, and a measurement model heavily centered on Google.

AI search gives us something different: synthesized answers, outputs that can vary across sessions, influence that may happen without a click, and fragmented experiences across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Search features such as AI Overviews and AI Mode.

**Here’s one of the most important changes: a brand can now be surfaced, recommended, and materially influence a purchase decision in AI search without necessarily generating a click.
**

A user asks ChatGPT “what’s the best PR opportunity tool for a small agency managing B2B SaaS clients?”, reads the answer, learns about three options, forms a preference, and then searches the brand name directly on Google. Or types the URL. Or opens the app. A meaningful part of the decision process may have happened inside the AI platform, while the eventual conversion was often attributed elsewhere.

That aligns with concerns raised by respondents in [SEOFOMO’s Organic Search Trends survey](https://hub.seofomo.co/surveys/organic-search-trends/) around AI attribution and trust, reflecting how difficult it is to assess AI influence when many journeys do not produce directly attributable clicks.

So we need a different model. One that doesn’t confuse measurable with meaningful, and doesn’t throw out commercial accountability just because attribution is harder. 

Here’s a framework that doesn’t claim complete attribution, isn’t a substitute for CRM or product analytics, and doesn’t promise that AI visibility will always translate into measurable business impact. It’s a structured way to measure, diagnose, and prioritize in an environment where observability is partial, platform behavior differs, and influence often extends beyond directly attributable clicks.

## **The 3 layers of AI search success
**

I’ve been working with this framework across client engagements and my own brands, and it has consistently helped produce more meaningful decisions than a single dashboard approach.

It has three connected metric layers:

|   |   |   |   |

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

|   |   |   |   |

|   |   |   |   |

|   |   |   |   |

The point isn’t to run three disconnected audits. It’s that: 

**Presence tells you where the brand appears,
**

**Readiness tells you why it looks that way,
**

**and Business Impact tells you whether that visibility creates measurable value.
**

Each layer hands off a hypothesis to the next. That’s what turns three reports into one diagnostic.

A few principles before we go layer by layer:

**Readiness is not the same as visibility:** Strong structure doesn’t guarantee being surfaced.

**Visibility is not the same as impact:** Being mentioned doesn’t guarantee commercial value.

**Platforms and surfaces should usually be tracked separately:** don’t blend Google AI Overviews and AI Mode, and don’t automatically blend ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, or Copilot either. The interfaces, source behavior, link treatment, and measurement visibility differ enough that blending can hide useful signal.

**Not all prompts matter equally:** Commercial, comparative, and shortlist prompts will tend to carry more weight than generic educational queries.

**Not all metrics deserve the same confidence:** Observed, proxy, and modelled signals should remain separate and labelled.

**Measurement should lead to action:** If a metric can’t change a decision, it shouldn’t be on the dashboard.

Let’s go through each layer.

## **Layer 1. Presence: Is the brand actually appearing, and how?
**

**Presence answers the most immediate question: is the brand actually appearing in the AI answers that matter, and how is it being represented when it does?
**

Without this layer, teams run broad audits and ship generic optimizations without knowing what’s actually suppressing visibility. Presence is where the weakness becomes visible, by platform, prompt group, persona, product line, market, or source ecosystem.

**How to set up your AI presence measuring protocol
**

Start narrow and deliberate. The goal is a measuring protocol focused on commercial value and action, not a giant prompt library that is not representative and meaningful:

**Prioritize the top 2 – 3 AI platforms based on a mix of measurable AI referral traffic in your vertical, audience usage, and commercial relevance.** Besides taking into account your own site’s observable AI traffic, you can use tools like Similarweb, Semrush, and similar platforms to estimate which AI platforms appear to be driving more traffic in your sector and competitor set.

**Create prompt libraries that reflect the constraints real buyers actually use** in AI platforms, not traditional search keywords stretched into prompts.

**Prioritize analysis around high commercial intent and high influence prompt groups.** Discovery prompts matter, but shortlist and selection prompts are where deals are won or lost.

**Look for patterns over time, not single-run results,** because AI outputs vary by session and platform. A single run is an anecdote; a sample is a signal.

**Translate each visibility gap into a likely readiness diagnosis:** so Layer 1 hands off directly to Layer 2 rather than producing a scorecard that just sits there.

Build prompt libraries that reflect real buyer behavior

The most common prompt library mistakes I see look like this:

Treating prompts like keywords without context.

Only tracking “best X” prompts.

Tracking too few prompts to get a stable read.

Not tracking each market, language, product line, stage of customer journey and persona individually.

A poorly representative prompt library distorts what you measure and produces work that doesn’t drive value. A good one reflects how buyers actually discover, compare, validate, and choose, not what a keyword tool surfaces.

**Where to source prompts from:
**

Non-brand demand data.

Sales call transcripts and support conversations.

Reviews and community language (Reddit, Slack groups, industry forums).

AI research tools’ sample data (Profound, Semrush).

[Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/) prompt and AI traffic samples for your site and competitors, where available.

[Bing Webmaster Tools](https://www.bing.com/webmasters) AI Performance report data, including citations, cited pages, and sampled grounding queries across supported Microsoft AI experiences.

Your top ranked underperforming long-tail queries from Google Search Console.

Then group them by target market/language, product or service line, customer journey stage, audience/persona, and buyer constraint, and add realistic persona, product-line, market, and constraint variants.

*Method note: not every KPI in this framework is equally objective. Some are directly measurable from platform or analytics data; others require a documented scoring protocol, repeated sampling, and human review. Treat recommendation rate, comparative win rate, and representation accuracy as structured decision support metrics rather than platform native ground truth. Report them separately and label confidence clearly. 
*

For any scored KPI, document the rubric, sample size, review cadence, and whether the outputs were assessed by one reviewer or calibrated across multiple reviewers.

**Here’s an example of how they work:
**

**Prompt coverage:** If you track 100 relevant prompts and the brand appears in 42, **prompt coverage = 42%**.

**Recommendation rate:** Out of 40 prompts where the brand appears, the AI explicitly recommends it in 18, **recommendation rate = 45%**.

**Linked citation rate:** The brand appears in 30 answers, and in 12 the AI includes a clickable link to the site, **linked citation rate = 40%**.

**Comparative win rate:** Across 20 comparison prompts like “What’s better for digital PR teams, Finchling or Google Alerts?”, the AI favors Finchling in 11 responses, **comparative win rate = 55%**.

**Representation accuracy:** The AI mentions Finchling in 25 answers with 20 of them being accurate (platform for reactive and proactive PR opportunities), 5 incorrect (generic media monitoring tool), **representation accuracy = 80%**.

Measured on their own, each number is interesting. Read together, they’re a diagnosis.

### **Which presence KPIs should lead your dashboard? It depends on your business model
**

Not every business should lead with the same metric. The same visibility gap has a different commercial meaning for a publisher than for a SaaS platform than for an ecommerce brand.

**Transactional sites (ecommerce, marketplaces, bookings):** Often lead with **linked citation rate** and **comparative win rate**, especially on platforms and prompt types where links are surfaced clearly enough to support click-out behavior. Revenue depends on click-capable mentions and winning selection-stage prompts like “best running shoes under $150” or “cheapest flights to Lisbon”.

**Lead gen and service sites (agencies, local services, consultancies):** Lead with **recommendation rate** and **comparative win rate**. The buyer journey is consultative: being actively endorsed for provider-selection prompts like “best PR agencies for SaaS” is the signal that matters.

**SaaS and product-led businesses:** Lead with **recommendation rate**, **comparative win rate**, and **representation accuracy**. Crowded, comparison-heavy categories where being framed correctly is as important as being surfaced.

**Publications and media sites:** Lead with **linked citation rate** and **prompt coverage**. The business model depends on referral traffic and being treated as an authoritative source.

**Informational, educational, or nonprofit sites:** Lead with **prompt coverage** and **representation accuracy**. Success is being reliably surfaced for the right topics with the right information.

If you’re unsure which KPI should lead, ask yourself these questions:

Does revenue require a click, or can value be created by the AI mention alone?

Is the category comparison heavy, or discovery heavy?

Are commercial and selection stage prompts where the money is, or do informational and discovery prompts drive most of the pipeline?

Is the brand sold directly on-site, through partners, through marketplaces, or offline; and therefore how much does linked citation rate actually matter?

Is being described correctly commercially critical, or is any mention a net positive?

**Which metric, if it moved by 20% next quarter, would most plausibly change the business outcome: sessions, pipeline, revenue, recall, or authority?
**

That last question is the one that matters most.

The metric that answers “yes, moving this would change the business outcome” is the one that belongs at the top of your dashboard. Everything else is secondary.

### **Build the AI search presence dashboard and use it to answer real questions
**

You can build your own Presence dashboard using AI monitoring platforms such as Similarweb, Profound, Peec AI, Semrush, Sistrix, Waikay, or your own internal tracking setup. Choose based on which platforms, prompts, exports, citation data, and scoring workflows they actually support, since tool coverage and methodology differ.

What matters isn’t the tool. What matters is whether the dashboard answers these questions:

Where does the brand appear, and where is it silent? Which platforms, journey stages, personas, product lines, or markets show the widest gaps?

When the brand appears, is it genuinely recommended or merely listed among alternatives?

Are mentions click-capable, or do they stay trapped inside the AI answer with no link?

In head-to-head or shortlist prompts, does the brand win, tie, or lose — and against whom consistently?

Is the brand being described accurately, or is it misframed, outdated, or confused with another product?

Which third-party domains shape the outcomes, and where is the source ecosystem working against the brand?

## **Layer 2. Readiness: Are you structurally prepared to be surfaced?
**

**Readiness explains the structural reasons behind the visibility patterns surfaced in Layer 1 and identifies which issues are most likely limiting stronger AI search performance.
**

Without this layer, teams respond to visibility gaps with generic content or technical work that doesn’t address the real bottleneck. Readiness turns Presence findings into structural priorities that can actually move the needle.

### **Start from Presence findings, not from a blank audit
**

The first rule of Readiness assessment: don’t run a blanket audit. Start from the specific patterns Layer 1 surfaced.

Mentions without links often point to **Accessible, Extractable, or Fresh** gaps.

Weak category visibility often points to **Corroborated, Differentiated, or Useful** gaps.

Weak recommendation rate often points to **Credible, Corroborated, or Differentiated** gaps.

Poor representation accuracy often points to **Recognizable or Consistent** gaps.

Weak commercial visibility often points to **Transactable, Extractable, or Useful** gaps.

This is why Layer 1 hands off to Layer 2: **You’re not auditing everything, you’re testing the structural hypotheses Presence surfaced.
**

### **An AI search readiness assessment outcome example
**

Say your Presence dashboard shows the brand appears in 70% of “best PM tools for engineering” prompts but in only 12% of “[brand] vs competitors” head-to-head prompts, and in that 12% it’s framed as “a newer alternative” rather than on its actual differentiators.

That’s not a distribution problem since visibility exists upstream. It’s a **Differentiated + Corroborated + Credible** gap.

The brand is surfaceable but not positioned strongly enough in the third-party sources AI platforms weigh at the comparison stage. So Layer 2 work should focus on comparison-site pages, analyst coverage, and positioning consistency across G2/Capterra/review sites.

### **Prioritize with effort, not just impact
**

Once you record the assessment outcome score, evidence, affected segment, owner, effort, and likely effect on Stage 1 KPIs for each gap, you can then prioritize using:

***Likely impact on key visibility gap × commercial importance ÷ ease of implementation
***

The effort denominator is what makes the roadmap realistic: 

An outdated pricing page causing Transactable failures on *“cheapest PM tool”* prompts should ship this week.

A weak analyst coverage program reducing Credible signal on shortlist prompts should get funded and planned over 6-12 months, but it doesn’t block short-term wins.

That’s how you end up with roadmaps that never ship.

## **Layer 3. Business Impact: Is AI visibility translating into value?
**

**The goal of this layer is not perfect attribution. It’s an honest reporting model to support budget, planning, and prioritization decisions without over-claiming.
**

No current measurement stack delivers complete, clean AI search attribution across platforms, surfaces, and journeys.

Google and Microsoft now provide partial, but still incomplete, visibility into AI search behavior. Google documents that AI Overviews and AI Mode are included within Search Console’s overall Web Performance reporting rather than broken out as separate standalone reports. Microsoft now provides AI Performance reporting in Bing Webmaster Tools public preview with citation counts, cited pages, and sampled grounding queries.

What this layer delivers is a layered reading of observed data, directional proxies, and modelled estimates: kept separate, labelled by confidence, and reviewed on a cadence the business can act on.

If I could put one sentence in front of every CFO on this:

***Measured AI referral traffic is the floor, not the ceiling, of AI’s contribution. In other words, observed AI-referred sessions are a measurable subset of AI influence, not a complete measure of it.
***

***A large share of AI influenced conversions return through branded search or direct traffic after a user saw the brand in an AI answer and did not click. Reporting only observed AI referral sessions systematically understates impact.
***

That’s not an excuse, it’s a design constraint, and the three-layer confidence model below is how you report around it honestly.

### **Four Business Impact confidence layers that shouldn’t be blended
**

The single most common reporting failure in AI search measurement is collapsing direct evidence, directional proxies, and modelled estimates into one undifferentiated “AI impact” number. Once those layers blur together, a CFO asks one question about methodology and the whole construct falls apart.

Label every metric in your dashboard with its confidence layer:

|   |   |   |

| --- | --- | --- |

|   |   |   |

|   |   |   |

|   |   |   |

|   |   |   |

Each layer answers a different question. Treating them as one number makes all four less useful, not more.

**What this looks like in practice: a Finchling monthly report:
**

**Observed:** 1,820 AI-referred sessions, 6.1% trial start rate, 2.4x the organic benchmark.

**Proxy – own:** Branded *“Finchling”* search +22% QoQ; direct traffic to /features/reactive-pr +38% QoQ.

**Proxy – third party:** Similarweb estimates roughly 4,200 AI sessions for the period, versus 1,820 observed in GA4; within the PR tools peer set, estimated AI traffic share is 6% for Finchling versus 41% for Muck Rack and 22% for Prowly.

**Modelled:** Based on estimated incremental branded demand above baseline for the quarter, a 30% AI influence assumption, and historical branded search to pipeline conversion rates, estimated influenced pipeline ~€14K ARR. Caveat band attached.

It’s important to note how each line is reported separately with its confidence label, and never collapsed into a single “AI impact” figure.

### **Build the business impact observed layer
**

The observed layer starts in your web analytics stack and, where possible, extends through to CRM or downstream revenue reporting. The setup itself is manageable, but it is not enabled by default, and it loses usefulness quickly if nobody maintains the tracking rules, channel definitions, and reporting logic over time.

If you’re setting this up in GA4, Dana DiTomaso’s guides on **[How to Track and Report on Traffic from AI Tools](https://kpplaybook.com/resources/how-to-report-on-traffic-from-ai-tools-in-ga4/)** and **[AI Traffic Analysis: Building GA4 Audiences That Drive Decisions](https://kpplaybook.com/resources/ai-traffic-analytics-audience-analysis-ga4/)** are strong practical references to start.

In GA4, the cleanest approach is to create a dedicated **custom channel group** for AI traffic.

Go to **Admin → Data display → Channel groups → Create new**, duplicate your existing grouping, and add a channel such as **AI Search** or **AI Assistants** using a **Source matches regex** rule. Google explicitly documents using a GA4 custom channel group with regex-based rules to group AI assistant traffic for reporting.

The channel order matters. GA4 includes traffic in the first channel whose definition it matches, so an AI assistants channel should sit above Referral and any broader matching rules. If Referral appears first, eligible AI traffic may be classified there before your custom AI rule ever fires.

For the regex, use a maintained starter pattern, not a fixed “final” list. Google’s documentation provides an **[example regex for AI assistants](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13051316#zippy=%2Cin-this-article)**, but your version should be updated over time based on the actual referrers and URL patterns your property receives.

A practical version can include platforms such as ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, DeepSeek, Grok, You.com, Phind, and Mistral, but the exact coverage will depend on the referrers and URL patterns your own property is actually receiving. Google’s example uses broader pattern matching rather than relying only on a narrow set of exact hosts.

What this layer captures well:

Traffic from AI platforms that pass a usable referrer or source parameter can be measured directly in GA4 via the custom channel group.

Some AI platforms are more observable than others in GA4 because referrer behavior varies by product, browser, app handoff, and click path. Treat platform-level observability as variable, not guaranteed.

What this layer doesn’t capture cleanly:

Some AI driven visits will still collapse into Direct, Referral, or other channels because of app handoffs, copied URLs, privacy controls, or missing referrer data. So the observed layer is useful, but incomplete by definition.

Google AI Overviews and AI Mode are not cleanly exposed in GA4 as their own native standalone traffic source. Google documents that AI Overviews and AI Mode are included in Search Console’s overall search results performance reporting, but that still does not make them cleanly separable as a distinct traffic source in GA4.

**What to track in the observed layer:
**

AI Sessions by platform, landing page, device.

Engagement rate and average engagement time versus the organic benchmark. Google has said that clicks from search results pages with AI features can be higher quality, for example by users spending more time on site, but that should be validated against your own benchmarks rather than assumed across all platforms or experiences. If it consistently underperforms your benchmark, that can indicate a landing page mismatch, weak prompt fit, or lower-quality visibility than expected.

AI conversion rate and revenue per visit, segmented by platform where volume allows.

AI assisted conversions (data-driven attribution in GA4, or multi-touch in the CRM).

Top AI landing pages: The pages that are actually being cited. This list is likely the most useful insight for informing Layer 2 work.

Own-site proxies have higher trust but are inward-looking.

Third-party tools signals have lower trust but are the only window onto competitors and prompt-level behavior.

You need both.

**An important, easy to set survey based discovery proxy signal
**

There’s one survey question that earns its place and I recommend to add to your signup flow, demo request, or post-trial onboarding:

***“Before signing up, did you come across [brand] in an AI assistant or AI search experience, such as ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, or Google’s AI features?”
***

***Options: Yes / No / Not sure.
***

Place it after the core signup fields, not before. Optional. One question, no follow-up (the moment it becomes a mini-survey, completion rates collapse and you want to avoid that). 

Why it matters disproportionately: a rising “Yes” rate among users attributed to Direct or Branded Organic is one of the strongest first-party proxies that AI influence exists beyond what analytics can directly observe.

Users who arrive via branded search with a high “Yes” rate are the invisible AI influence. They’re attributed to Organic in GA4 but wouldn’t have searched the brand without an AI mention.

Users who arrive via Direct with a high “Yes” rate are the mobile-ChatGPT copy-paste cohort. GA4 attribution is entirely blind to them.

Users who arrive via the AI Search channel itself but answer “No” may include misattributed traffic: agency traffic, internal team members, secondary clicks.

**Third-party proxy reads worth running monthly
**

**[Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/)**, **[Semrush](https://www.semrush.com/)** and equivalent tools can fill gaps your own analytics can’t cover directly, although imperfectly. Data comes from panels, clickstream samples, and modelling, not from your logs. Use them for relative reads (us vs. competitors, this month vs. last, prompt A vs. prompt B) rather than absolute claims.

Here are 3 specific reads to run monthly:

**1. Prompt samples driving traffic to your top AI landing pages.
**

Similarweb surfaces a sample of the prompts that produced AI referred visits to specific URLs. Use it to extend your Presence prompt set, diagnose landing page mismatch, and inform Readiness work. 

**2. Competitive benchmarking of AI traffic share, top landing pages, and top prompts per page.
**

Track your estimated share of AI-referred sessions across a defined peer set over time. A rising share is usually a positive signal; a flat share during category growth may indicate relative loss. Identify which pages every competitor is getting AI traffic to (commodity pages) and which are distinctively yours (your moat).

**3. AI platform mix over time, benchmarked.
**

Spot platform specific decay (your ChatGPT share flat while a competitor’s doubles), platform specific wins (your Perplexity share disproportionately high, reverse-engineer what earned it), and category shifts (the whole competitor set losing ChatGPT share while AI Mode rises).

**Reading the proxies together: three common patterns
**

Proxies only become useful when interpreted as a pattern. Here are 3 readings that come up often:

**Scenario A: Hidden success.
**

AI referral sessions flat. Branded search +18%. Direct +9%. Survey “how did you hear about us” shows rising AI mentions. Third-party AI traffic share growing relative to peers.

**Reading:** visibility is working; users see the brand in AI answers but arrive via brand name or direct. Impact is real but hidden. Most common pattern for established brands.

**Move:** keep investing, and lean on survey data, branded search evidence, and competitive share in reporting rather than observed sessions alone.

**Scenario B: Traffic without fit.
**

AI referral sessions up. Branded search flat. Conversion rate from AI below organic benchmark. Third-party prompt samples show prompts driving traffic to pages not built for those prompts.

**Reading:** traffic is arriving but not qualified. Likely recommendation quality or landing page mismatch.

**Move:** audit the sampled prompts against their landing pages, and either redirect cited URLs to more specific pages or rewrite pages to match intent. Most fixable of the three and often the fastest QoQ improvement.

**Scenario C: Clean case.
**

AI referral sessions up. Branded search up. AI-assisted conversions visible. Survey signal rising. Third-party share up, platform mix diversifying.

**Reading:** observed, own-proxy, and third-party signals all point in the same direction. This is consistent with share gain rather than simply benefiting from category growth.

**Move:** scale investment, expand prompt coverage to adjacent clusters, and keep testing the estimate against future survey, branded-demand, and observed conversion trends.

### **Build the business impact modelled layer
**

The observed layer measures what you can see. The proxy layer signals what you can infer. The modelled layer estimates what you can’t measure directly but may still need a planning number for, typically when leadership asks “what is AI search actually worth to us?” and observed sessions alone understate the answer.

Modelled metrics aren’t a replacement for observed and proxy data. They’re a principled way of combining them into a planning number. The rigor comes from making the assumptions explicit, keeping the confidence band wide, and never presenting the output as proof.

**The baseline modelled estimate
**

The simplest version applies an assumption to a proxy signal:

***(Incremental branded clicks, visits, leads, or pipeline above baseline) × (stated AI influence assumption %) = modelled influenced value
***

For example, applied to Finchling: first estimate the incremental branded demand above baseline for the quarter, then apply a 30% AI influence assumption to that increment, then translate that influenced share into ARR using historical branded-search-to-pipeline or branded-search-to-ARR rates. That yields the modelled influenced pipeline range.

Inputs to combine:

**Branded search lift from GSC:** the clearest proxy most brands will have.

**Direct traffic lift to cited pages:** useful where mobile to direct AI journeys are common.

**Survey AI discovery rate:** often the strongest first-party anchor for the AI influence assumption, because it grounds the estimate in observed user reported behavior.

**Historical conversion value per visit or lead:** to translate sessions into commercial terms.

How to choose and justify the attribution assumption:

**Start from the survey “Yes” rate among users arriving via branded search or direct.** If roughly 30% of relevant new signups report seeing the brand in an AI assistant, that can be used as a reasonable starting assumption, provided the sample size, response rate, and wording are stable enough to compare over time.

**Cross-check against third-party AI traffic share.** If branded search, survey-based AI discovery, and external AI traffic indicators rise together, confidence in the assumption increases. If they diverge, confidence decreases and the estimate should be discounted.

**Document what you excluded.** Product launches, paid campaigns, or PR moments in the same window should come off the top.

**How to report it:
**

Always as a range, never a single number.

Always with the stated attribution assumption, inputs, exclusions, and timeframe clearly documented.

Always below the observed and proxy numbers in the dashboard, not above them.

A reportable line looks like:

*“Modelled influenced pipeline for Q1: €12–16K ARR, based on an estimated increment in branded demand above baseline for the quarter, a 30% AI-influence assumption applied to that increment, and historical branded-search-to-pipeline conversion rates, cross-checked against rising survey discovery rate (27% → 34%) and stable third-party AI share.”
*

**What the modelled layer captures well
**

A planning number that accounts for AI influence invisible to observed tracking.

A way to translate directional proxy signals into commercial terms leadership can use for budget conversations.

A disciplined alternative to either ignoring AI influence because it can’t be measured cleanly, or overclaiming it by crediting AI for every branded search lift.

**What the modelled layer doesn’t capture cleanly
**

A modelled estimate should be treated as a planning construct, not as attributed revenue.

**Platform-specific attribution. **The assumption applies across AI search as a whole.

**Short-term movements. **Modelled estimates stabilize over quarters, not weeks.

**What to track
**

Modelled influenced pipeline or revenue, stated as a range with inputs documented.

Attribution percentage applied over time, tracked alongside survey discovery rate so the two move together.

**Sensitivity band:** What the number looks like at ±10 percentage points of attribution %, so leadership sees how much depends on the assumption.

Refresh quarterly, not monthly: The inputs are too noisy below that cadence. Re-validate the attribution percentage every two quarters against the survey response rate, and retire the estimate entirely if the inputs become unreliable. A modelled number built on a broken input is worse than no number at all.

**Something to remind:** this is for planning, never for proof.

The moment it gets cited as a defensible attribution figure rather than a working assumption, it stops being useful and starts eroding trust in the whole dashboard.

### **An example with Finchling insights
**

**Base reading:
**

Presence dashboard shows 58% prompt coverage in ChatGPT for discovery prompts but 11% recommendation rate in shortlist prompts.

Readiness shows Differentiated and Credible scoring well, but Corroborated scoring low (few third-party reviews, limited presence on roundup sites).

Business Impact shows flat AI referral traffic and slightly rising branded search.

**Matrix read:** “high readiness + low visibility” at the commercial end of the funnel.

**Diagnosis:** much of the structural work appears to be in place. The bottleneck is source ecosystem presence at the comparison stage. AI models have nowhere to learn about Finchling in the context of selection prompts because Finchling is not in the sources they cite for those prompts.

**Move:** There should be a concentrated effort on getting Finchling onto software roundup pages, G2 and Capterra category pages, and reactive PR tool comparisons. Not more content. Not more technical optimization. The lever in this case is external corroboration.

That’s what tying the layers together gives you: targeted recommendations and actions to close the existing AI search gaps that will drive business impact.

## **Where to start: the 3 layer framework minimum viable setup
**

The framework scales to a full enterprise program, but a lean version can often be made operational in about two weeks.

**Week 1: Baseline.
**

Define priority platforms, competitors, personas, product lines, markets.

Build 50-70 priority prompts across Discovery, Evaluation, Selection, Post-purchase.

Run the first visibility baseline (5–7 runs per prompt per platform).

Identify top cited domains and biggest source-ecosystem gaps.

Define Layer 1 KPI set and dashboard shell.

**Week 2: Connect the layers.
**

Translate the three biggest Layer 1 gaps into Readiness hypotheses.

Run a targeted readiness audit on those hypotheses only.

Set up the GA4 AI referrer channel group.

Add the one AI discovery question to signup, demo, or post-purchase flows.

Define the weekly, monthly, quarterly review rhythm.

Assign first actions and owners.

You should end week two with:

A visibility baseline by platform and stage (one number per cell, sample size documented)

A top 10 list of third-party domains shaping category answers

Three Readiness hypotheses with owners and target dates

A functioning AI channel group in GA4

A live discovery question in at least one acquisition flow

A scheduled monthly review with the right three or four people in the room.

The framework scales from here.

## **The takeaway
**

Redefining success metrics for the AI search era means measuring performance across three layers:

**Presence** tells you whether and how the brand appears.

**Readiness** tells you whether the structural conditions for stronger visibility are in place.

**Business Impact** tells you whether that visibility is creating measurable value.

Measurement in AI search isn’t about more dashboards. It’s about connecting **where** the brand appears, **why** it appears that way, and **whether** it matters commercially, and being willing to act on what that connection reveals.

Teams that run the three layers in isolation are more likely to ship disconnected work. Teams that run them together will know which lever to pull next.

Pick your metrics based on business importance. Report them with the right segmentation and confidence level. Interpret them with the right questions. And act on them by closing the structural, source, representation, or conversion gaps they expose.

It’s time to measure AI search in a way that supports better decisions.

# **[AI Search Technical Checklist](https://community.searchleaders.ai/c/tools-resources/aeo-technical-checklist)****
**

[Jeremy Cabral](https://community.searchleaders.ai/u/1f2b9e14)

Admin🏅Founding Member

[5d
](https://community.searchleaders.ai/c/tools-resources/aeo-technical-checklist)

Founder of AI Search Leaders

Two versions in one document. The Quick Scan is a tiered 20-point field guide for any AI Search audit - each item includes the exact check to run and what a fail means for citation rate. The Deep Audit covers the same items with full context, tools, and fixes.

This checklist is built around what actually drives citation rate in practice - including the checks most practitioners miss entirely.

## **Part 1: Quick Scan
**

Run in under 5 minutes. Each item tells you exactly how to check it. Items marked [DEV] need developer involvement to fix.

## **Tier 1: Critical Blockers
**

If any of these fail, everything else is secondary. These prevent citation regardless of content quality.

1. Run "Describe [brand] in one paragraph" on Claude, ChatGPT, and Perplexity. This is the most important check in the audit and most practitioners skip it entirely. Read each output carefully. Compare it to how the brand actually describes itself. The gap between those two things is your entire AI Search problem statement - and it tells you where to start. Failing means: You are optimizing without knowing what you are optimizing against.

2. AI crawlers not blocked in robots.txt - and verified in server logs. Check: Go to [domain]/robots.txt. Search for GPTBot, PerplexityBot, anthropic-ai, Bytespider, CCBot, Meta-ExternalAgent, and Applebot-Extended. None should be followed by Disallow: /. Then verify in Cloudflare, server logs, or a crawler analytics tool that these bots are actually visiting - robots.txt compliance is not guaranteed. Failing means: The content cannot be indexed or cited by the platforms you are optimizing for, regardless of how good it is.

3. Key content renders without JavaScript. [DEV] Check: Open a core content page. In Chrome, go to DevTools > Settigs > Disable JavaScript. Reload. Is the main body content - headings, paragraphs, the actual information - still visible without JS executing? Failing means: AI crawlers that do not run JavaScript see an empty shell. This is the most underdiagnosed AI Search blocker because it is invisible to human visitors.

4. Search the brand on Perplexity and check which domains appear as citations. Check: Run 5 to 10 core category queries on Perplexity. Note which domains appear as cited sources in the responses. Is the brand's domain among them? Which competitor domains appear most frequently? Failing means: You now know exactly which sites LLMs trust in this category - and whether yours is one of them. This is your citation gap map.

5. Check if the brand's content appears in Common Crawl. Check: Go to[ ](http://commoncrawl.org/)[commoncrawl.org](http://commoncrawl.org/) and use the index search to check whether the brand's domain appears in recent crawl data. Common Crawl is one of the primary training data sources for most LLMs. Failing means: If the domain is not in Common Crawl, it is likely absent or underrepresented in LLM training data entirely - which means no amount of on-page optimization will close the gap without increasing crawl accessibility.

## **Tier 2: Citation Rate Drivers
**

These are the factors that determine whether a site gets cited as a source, not just mentioned by brand name. Mention rate and citation rate are different metrics. Most brands optimize for mentions. Citations are what drive conversion.

6. The brand has at least one named framework or methodology. Check: Does the brand own a specific, named concept - a framework, a model, a methodology - that is referenced by its name? Not just a product feature, but a named way of thinking about a problem. Why it matters: LLMs disproportionately cite named frameworks because they are unambiguous reference points. "The LLMO Content Pyramid" gets cited more than "how to structure content for LLM citation." If the brand does not have one, that is the highest-leverage content investment available.

7. Quotable paragraph audit on key pages. Check: Read the first 200 words of each key page. Identify any sentence that could be extracted and cited verbatim without context - a specific claim, a clear definition, a data point with attribution. Count how many quotable sentences exist. Why it matters: LLMs pull short, self-contained statements. Pages written in flowing editorial prose are harder to cite than pages with clear, extractable claims. Most brand content fails this test badly.

8. Original data published and confirmed cited by others. Check: Does the brand have at least one piece of content presenting data no one else has? Then check: is that data being cited by third-party publications and practitioners? Use Perplexity to search for the data point and see if it surfaces with the brand as the source. Why it matters: Citation rate for original data is 5 to 10 times higher than for content that aggregates others' research. Being the source is the only way to become a default reference in LLM responses.

9. Brand mentioned in Wikipedia articles about the category - not just having its own Wikipedia page. Check: Search Wikipedia for the category (e.g. "AI search optimization," "answer engine optimization," the relevant product category). Is the brand mentioned within those articles? Why it matters: Wikipedia is one of the heaviest-weighted sources in LLM training. Being referenced within a Wikipedia article about your category is a stronger entity signal than having your own Wikipedia page. Most brands focus on getting their own page and miss this.

10. Google AI Overviews appearing for core category queries. Check: Search 5 to 10 primary category queries in Google. Does an AI Overview appear? Is the brand referenced? Which competitor brands appear? Why it matters: Google's AI Overviews draw from similar authority signals to other LLMs. A brand that does not appear in AI Overviews is likely struggling across all AI search platforms. Appearing in AI Overviews is a strong leading indicator of broader LLM citation presence.

11. FAQ content sourced from real user queries, not internal guesses. Check: Compare the FAQ questions on key pages against the People Also Ask results on Google for the same topic. Do they match? Or are the FAQs written from the brand's perspective rather than the user's? Why it matters: FAQs written to match how users actually ask questions get extracted more reliably. Internally-generated FAQs that do not match real query language are nearly invisible to LLMs.

12. Measure citation rate, not just mention rate. Check: Run 20 queries across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, and Gemini. For each response that mentions the brand, check whether the brand's URL is cited as a source (in Perplexity this is visible; in ChatGPT and Claude you may need to ask the LLM to cite sources). Calculate: of responses mentioning the brand, what percentage also cite the brand's URL? Why it matters: A brand can have 70% mention rate and 5% citation rate. That means LLMs know the brand exists but do not consider their content authoritative enough to reference directly. Mention rate feels good. Citation rate is what drives traffic and trust.

## **Tier 3: Entity Authority
**

These build the underlying entity model that LLMs use to understand and frame the brand. Slower to change but foundational.

13. Entity consistency audit across all external profiles. Check: Compare the brand description across: Organization schema on homepage, LinkedIn company page, Crunchbase, G2 or Capterra (if applicable), Wikipedia (if it exists), and the About page. Are they describing the same company in the same terms? Note any contradictions in founding year, employee count, product description, or market positioning. Why it matters: LLMs build entity models by aggregating signals across sources. Conflicting descriptions create uncertainty. The LLM averages them into a muddier, less confident description of the brand - which reduces citation confidence.

14. Organization schema sameAs links to every official profile. Check: Inspect Organization schema on homepage. Does sameAs include LinkedIn, Crunchbase, Wikipedia (if applicable), official social profiles, and any relevant industry directories? Are all URLs live and pointing to the correct pages? Why it matters: sameAs links are how LLMs confirm entity identity across sources. Missing links mean the LLM cannot fully consolidate its entity model. Every official profile not listed is a missed authority signal.

15. Author Person schema linked from content and connected to verifiable external profiles. Check: Click the author byline on a key piece of content. Does it link to a bio page with Person schema? Does that schema include sameAs links to LinkedIn and any published work? Is the same author entity used consistently across the site? Why it matters: Content authored by identifiable, verifiable experts is given higher credibility weight. The connection between a piece of content and a credible human identity is a key AI Search signal that most brands implement partially or inconsistently.

16. dateModified in Article schema is actually updating when content is edited. Check: Find a piece of content and note the dateModified in its Article schema (use View Source or Google's Rich Results Test). Ask: when was this page last meaningfully edited? Do the dates match? Many CMS implementations set dateModified once at publication and never update it automatically. Why it matters: Stale dateModified signals tell LLMs the content is old even if it was updated last week. This is a common and easy-to-miss implementation error.

17. Internal linking creates topical clusters around the brand's highest-priority AI Search pages. Check: Go to the page the brand most wants to be cited for. How many internal links point to it from relevant pages? Is it the authoritative center of a topic cluster, or is it an orphan that receives no internal link support? Why it matters: LLMs infer topical authority partly from how a site organizes itself. A page that is heavily linked internally signals that the site considers it authoritative. An isolated page - even if the content is excellent - has weaker signals.

18. Heading structure mirrors how users actually ask questions. Check: Extract all H2 and H3 headings from a key page. Search each one on Google and check if a similar phrase appears in People Also Ask results. If headings are internal labels ("Our Approach," "Key Considerations") rather than natural language queries or answer summaries, they are not doing AI Search work. Why it matters: LLMs use heading structure to match page sections to query intent. A heading that sounds like a user's question is more likely to be surfaced as an answer to that question.

19. Content structure passes the inverted pyramid test. Check: Read only the first paragraph of a key page. After that one paragraph, can a reader extract a clear, complete answer to the page's primary question? Test this by showing the first paragraph to someone unfamiliar with the brand and asking what the page is about. Why it matters: LLMs prioritize extracting answers from content that leads with the answer. Pages that build context before reaching the point are less citable than pages that answer first and elaborate second.

20. The brand's position in LLM responses tracked over time - not just whether it appears, but where. Check: In your monthly query testing, record not just whether the brand appears but its position in the response - first brand named, named alongside others, named late, or not named. Track this monthly. Is the brand moving earlier in responses over time? Why it matters: Being named fifth in a list of six tools is categorically different from being named first. Most AI Search measurement tracks presence. The practitioners who track position have a fundamentally more accurate picture of how AI search is shifting - and can catch momentum changes before they show up in traffic data.

## **Part 2: Deep Audit
**

Same 20 items with full context - what it is, why it matters, how to check it properly, and how to fix it.

## **Tier 1: Critical Blockers
**

### **1. Run "Describe [brand] in one paragraph" on Claude, ChatGPT, and Perplexity
**

What it is: A direct audit of how the three most-used AI search platforms currently understand and describe the brand - pulled straight from the source.

Why it matters for AI Search: Most AI Search audits start with robots.txt and schema. This one starts by reading what the LLM actually thinks, because that is the ground truth you are trying to shift. Every other check in this audit is in service of changing this output. If you do not run this check first, you are optimizing without knowing what you are optimizing against.

The outputs often reveal things brands do not expect. Common findings: the LLM describes a brand in terms of a product they launched five years ago and have since pivoted from. The LLM associates the brand with a competitor more prominent in its training data. The LLM knows the brand name but has thin knowledge of what it actually does. The LLM describes the brand's positioning accurately but not in the language the brand uses - which means the brand is winning in AI search but not getting credit for it in client conversations.

How to check: Open a fresh conversation in each platform. Paste: "Describe [brand name] in one paragraph, based on what you know from your training data. Be honest about what you know and do not know." Compare the three outputs. Note where they agree, where they diverge, and where they contradict the brand's actual positioning. Use the diagnosis categories from the Brand Intelligence Audit to categorize the problem.

How to fix: This check is diagnostic, not directly fixable. The fixes come from everything else in this checklist. But the output of this check should become the brief for all AI Search content work - specifically, the gap between the current LLM description and the desired description is what needs to close.

### **2. AI crawlers not blocked - and verified in server logs
**

What it is: A full audit of which AI crawlers are allowed to index the site, verified both in robots.txt and in actual server log data.

Why it matters for AI Search: robots.txt compliance is not guaranteed. Some crawlers respect it; others do not. More importantly, many brands have wildcard disallow rules (User-agent: * / Disallow: /) that block AI crawlers by default, then add selective allow rules for Googlebot - but never explicitly allow AI crawlers. The result is a site that appears accessible in robots.txt review but blocks every major AI crawler in practice.

The full list of AI crawlers to allow (as of 2025): GPTBot (OpenAI), PerplexityBot (Perplexity), anthropic-ai (Anthropic/Claude), Google-Extended (Gemini), Bytespider (ByteDance), CCBot (Common Crawl), Meta-ExternalAgent (Meta AI), Applebot-Extended (Apple), Diffbot, and Amazonbot. Most checklists only mention GPTBot. That leaves most of the major platforms unaddressed.

How to check: Step 1 - Go to [domain]/robots.txt. For each crawler in the list above, check: is there an explicit allow rule? Is there any disallow rule that would catch it? Look for wildcard User-agent: * rules with broad disallow paths.

Step 2 - Verify actual crawl activity in server logs or Cloudflare analytics. Filter by user agent strings for each major AI crawler. Are they making requests? How frequently? Which pages are they hitting?

How to fix: Add explicit allow rules for each AI crawler you want to permit. Place them above any wildcard rules to ensure they take precedence. If the team has concerns about content scraping, consider allowing crawlers selectively by path rather than blanket blocking. Review this quarterly - AI crawler user agent strings change as platforms update.

### **3. Key content renders without JavaScript
**

What it is: A test to confirm that the primary body content of key pages is present in the initial HTML response, before any JavaScript executes.

Why it matters for AI Search: Single-page application frameworks (React, Vue, Next.js without SSR, Angular) often deliver a minimal HTML shell on the first request and then populate content via JavaScript. AI crawlers that do not execute JavaScript - including some configurations of GPTBot and most secondary crawlers - receive only the shell. The crawl shows pages as blank or near-blank, even though human visitors see full content.

This is the most underdiagnosed AI Search technical blocker. It is invisible to anyone auditing the site as a human visitor. It requires deliberate testing to catch.

How to check: Open a key content page in Chrome. Open DevTools (Cmd+Option+I or F12). Click the gear icon for Settings. Under Preferences, check "Disable JavaScript." Close Settings. Reload the page. The content visible in this state is what most AI crawlers see. Repeat for 5 to 10 key pages. Pay particular attention to: the main body text, headings, and any FAQs or structured content.

Also: use curl to fetch the page and inspect the raw HTML. Run: curl -s [URL] | grep -c "[a-z]" to get a rough word count of the initial HTML. Compare to the visible word count on the page. A large discrepancy indicates JavaScript-dependent content.

How to fix: Implement server-side rendering (SSR) or static site generation (SSG) for content pages. The fix ensures the full content is included in the initial HTML response. Next.js (React), Nuxt.js (Vue), and SvelteKit all support SSR natively. This is a developer-level change that may require architectural decisions about the site. Prioritize content pages - blog, resources, product documentation - over app or dashboard pages.

### **4. Search the brand on Perplexity and map which domains get cited
**

What it is: A live audit of which sources Perplexity cites when answering questions in the brand's category - and whether the brand's domain is among them.

Why it matters for AI Search: Perplexity shows its citations visibly, which makes it the most transparent window into which sites LLMs actually trust in a given category. The domains that appear as Perplexity citations are the sites LLMs have established as authoritative references. If the brand's domain is not appearing there, the problem is not visibility - it is authority.

This check also reveals the competitive citation landscape: which competitor domains are being cited most? What types of content are getting cited (blog posts, research reports, product pages, press coverage)? This tells you exactly what kind of content you need to produce to enter the citation set.

How to check: Open Perplexity. Run 10 queries that cover: 2 problem-aware queries, 2 category queries, 2 comparison queries, 2 expert recommendation queries, and 2 queries that name the brand directly. For each response, note: which domains appear as citations, what type of content they are, and whether the brand's domain appears. Build a simple table: Query | Citations | Brand domain cited (Y/N).

How to fix: This check is diagnostic. The output tells you which content types to prioritize. If competitor blogs are being cited, build a blog with deeper content. If research reports are being cited, produce original research. If press coverage is driving citations, prioritize PR in the publications appearing. The goal is to match the content profile of the sites already in the citation set.

### **5. Check if the brand's content appears in Common Crawl
**

What it is: A check to verify whether the brand's domain appears in Common Crawl - one of the largest open-source web crawl datasets and a primary training data source for most major LLMs.

Why it matters for AI Search: If a brand's content is not in Common Crawl, it is likely absent or significantly underrepresented in LLM training data. This explains cases where a brand has good content, no crawler blocks, and strong on-page signals - but still does not appear in LLM responses. The content simply was not in the data the model was trained on.

Common Crawl is not a crawl you can directly influence in real time, but checking for presence gives important diagnostic signal, particularly for newer domains or domains that have recently launched significant content.

How to check: Go to[ ](http://index.commoncrawl.org/collinfo.json)[index.commoncrawl.org/collinfo.json](http://index.commoncrawl.org/collinfo.json) to find recent crawl indices. Use the Common Crawl Index Server to query a specific domain:[ ](http://index.commoncrawl.org/CC-MAIN-%5BCRAWL-ID%5D-index?url=%5Bdomain%5D&output=json)[http://index.commoncrawl.org/CC-MAIN-[CRAWL-ID]-index?url=[domain]&output=json](http://index.commoncrawl.org/CC-MAIN-%5BCRAWL-ID%5D-index?url=%5Bdomain%5D&output=json). Check the most recent 3 to 4 crawls. Note how many pages from the domain appear and which pages specifically.

Alternatively, use third-party tools like CDX Toolkit or the Common Crawl search interface to check domain presence without direct API queries.

How to fix: Ensure the domain is crawlable (no crawler blocks, no JS dependency issues, fast page speed, valid sitemap submitted). Common Crawl runs periodic large-scale crawls - improving crawlability increases the likelihood of inclusion in the next crawl cycle. For new domains or domains with recent major content additions, this lag between publishing and LLM training data inclusion is expected. The fix is patience plus crawlability.

## **Tier 2: Citation Rate Drivers
**

### **6. The brand has at least one named framework or methodology
**

What it is: A proprietary, named concept - a framework, a model, a methodology, a named approach - that is attributed to the brand and referenced by that specific name.

Why it matters for AI Search: LLMs disproportionately cite named frameworks because they are unambiguous reference points. "The Inbound Marketing methodology" gets cited as attributed to HubSpot. "The Jobs to be Done framework" gets cited as attributed to Clayton Christensen. "The Flywheel" gets cited as attributed to Amazon. Generic content about "how to do content marketing" does not accumulate citations in the same way.

A named framework is a high leverage AI Search content investment for most brands, because it creates a citation anchor that is uniquely associated with the brand by name. Every time an LLM mentions the framework, it mentions the brand.

How to check: Ask: does the brand have any named frameworks, models, or methodologies that practitioners in the category use and reference by name? Search the brand name plus "framework" or "methodology" on Perplexity. Does anything come up? Search the framework name on its own - does the brand appear as the originator?

How to fix: Identify a genuine insight the brand has that is not yet named. It should be: non-obvious, specific to the brand's area of expertise, and useful enough that practitioners would want to reference it. Name it. Write a definitive piece of content introducing and explaining it. Distribute it aggressively. The goal is for practitioners to start referencing it by name - which then enters LLM training data as an attributed concept.

### **7. Quotable paragraph audit
**

What it is: An assessment of whether key pages contain self-contained, extractable sentences that an LLM can cite without surrounding context.

Why it matters for AI Search: LLMs pull short, specific statements when building their responses. "According to [Brand], the primary reason AI Search fails is that brands optimize for mentions rather than citations" is citable. A paragraph that spends 80 words establishing context before making a point is not. Most brand content fails this test because it is written for human readers who have context - not for LLMs that need self-contained claims.

The specific pattern to look for: does each key page have at least 3 sentences in the first 300 words that could be extracted and cited in isolation, without any surrounding context?

How to check: Read the first 300 words of each key page. For each sentence, ask: if this sentence appeared alone in an LLM response attributed to [brand], would it make complete sense? Would it be useful? Would it be credible? Count how many sentences pass. A page with 0 to 1 quotable sentences in the opening has a serious AI Search problem regardless of overall content quality.

How to fix: Rewrite key pages to front-load quotable claims. Useful formats: specific statistics ("Brands with original research are cited X times more often than brands that aggregate data from others"), named definitions ("[Term] is [clear, specific definition in one sentence]"), contrarian insights ("[Common belief] is wrong - here is what the data actually shows"), and named frameworks with specific explanations. Every key page should have at least 3 immediately citable sentences in the opening.

### **8. Original data published and confirmed cited by others
**

What it is: Original research or proprietary data that the brand has published AND that has been cited by third-party publications, practitioners, or other sites.

Why it matters for AI Search: Publishing original data is necessary but not sufficient. The data needs to be cited by others to enter LLM training data as an attributed fact. A survey published on a brand's blog that no one links to or references has limited AI Search value. The same survey, cited in ten industry publications, becomes a persistent reference point in LLM responses.

How to check: Step 1 - Does the brand have any original data published? Step 2 - Search for specific data points from that research on Perplexity. Is the brand cited as the source? Step 3 - Check backlinks to the research piece using Ahrefs or similar. How many external sites link to it? What publications? Step 4 - Run the data point as a query in ChatGPT. Does the model know the statistic? Does it attribute it to the brand?

How to fix: If no original data exists, commission a survey. If data exists but is not cited, the distribution strategy failed - re-launch the research as a PR asset, pitch it to relevant publications, send it to practitioners who might reference it, and brief it to relevant journalists. The goal is to get the specific data point attributed to the brand in at least 10 to 15 external publications. That is the threshold at which it starts appearing reliably in LLM training data.

### **9. Brand mentioned within Wikipedia articles about the category
**

What it is: A check for whether the brand appears as a reference within existing Wikipedia articles covering the relevant category, industry, or topic area.

Why it matters for AI Search: Most people think of Wikipedia AI Search as "does the brand have its own Wikipedia page." The more important question is: is the brand mentioned within the Wikipedia articles that LLMs draw from when understanding the category? Wikipedia is one of the most heavily weighted sources in LLM training. A brand mentioned in the Wikipedia article on "answer engine optimization" or "search engine marketing" is signaling category relevance to LLMs in a way that the brand's own website cannot replicate.

How to check: Search Wikipedia for the primary category, adjacent categories, and any named methodologies the brand is associated with. Open each relevant article. Use Ctrl+F to search for the brand name. Is it there? How is it described? Is the description accurate?

How to fix: If the brand is not mentioned where it reasonably should be, contribute to the Wikipedia article. Wikipedia contributions must follow neutral point of view guidelines - adding the brand as an example where it genuinely belongs is appropriate. Adding promotional language is not. If the brand's absence from a Wikipedia article is due to low notability in that category, the fix is building more third-party coverage until the notability threshold is met.

### **10. Google AI Overviews appearing for core category queries
**

What it is: A check for whether the brand appears in Google's AI Overviews (formerly Search Generative Experience) for primary category and comparison queries.

Why it matters for AI Search: Google AI Overviews draw from similar authority signals to other LLMs - E-E-A-T, structured data, cited third-party content, and original expertise. A brand that appears in AI Overviews is demonstrating the same signals that drive citation in ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Claude. Conversely, a brand absent from AI Overviews will almost certainly be absent or weak across other AI search platforms. This makes AI Overviews a fast, accessible proxy for broader AI Search health.

How to check: Search 10 category and comparison queries in Google (use a logged-out browser to avoid personalization). Record: does an AI Overview appear? Is the brand named in it? Which competitor brands appear? Which sources are cited in the "Learn more" section? Do this monthly - AI Overview appearance is volatile and tracking changes tells you whether your AI Search signals are improving.

How to fix: AI Overview appearance is driven by the same signals as all other AI Search: authoritative content, strong entity signals, cited sources, and structured data. There is no specific Google AI Overviews optimization separate from general AI Search. If the brand is not appearing, prioritize the Tier 1 and Tier 2 items in this checklist.

### **11. FAQ content sourced from real user queries
**

What it is: An audit of whether FAQ content on key pages uses the same language real users use when asking questions - or whether it is written from the brand's internal perspective.

Why it matters for AI Search: LLMs match FAQ content to queries based on language similarity. An FAQ question written as "What are the key differentiators of our platform?" will not match a user query of "how is this different from competitors?" An FAQ question written as "How does [brand] compare to [competitor]?" will. The source of FAQ questions matters as much as the format.

How to check: Copy the FAQ questions from a key page. Search the same page topic on Google and check the People Also Ask section. Do the FAQ questions match the PAA questions? Run the FAQ questions as queries in Perplexity - do they surface as natural queries? Compare FAQ language to actual customer support tickets or sales call recordings if accessible.

How to fix: Replace internally-generated FAQ questions with questions sourced from: People Also Ask results on Google, actual customer support questions, sales call transcripts, Reddit threads in relevant subreddits, and Quora questions in the relevant category. The questions should sound like something a real person would type or say - not something a product manager would write.

### **12. Measure citation rate, not just mention rate
**

What it is: A monthly measurement of what percentage of LLM responses that mention the brand also cite the brand's URL as a source - rather than just tracking whether the brand name appears in responses.

Why it matters for AI Search: Mention rate and citation rate are different metrics that tell different stories. A brand with 70% mention rate and 8% citation rate is well-known but not trusted as a source - LLMs know the brand exists but do not consider its content authoritative enough to reference directly. A brand with 40% mention rate and 35% citation rate has a content trust problem (not enough awareness) but strong source authority for the audience it does reach. The distinction shapes the entire AI Search strategy.

Most AI Search measurement focuses on mention rate because it is easy to track. Citation rate requires checking whether URLs appear in responses - which is visible in Perplexity (citations are shown) and can be tested in ChatGPT by asking the model to cite sources.

How to check: In your monthly query set, for every response that mentions the brand, check: does the response also include a URL from the brand's domain as a cited source? In Perplexity, this is visible in the sidebar. In ChatGPT, add "Please cite your sources with URLs" to each query. In Claude, sources are not always shown but can be requested. Calculate: (responses citing brand URL) divided by (responses mentioning brand name) = citation rate.

How to fix: Citation rate improves through: original data that has been distributed and cited by third parties, named frameworks referenced by practitioners, content that appears in authoritative publications (which then becomes a training data signal), and FAQ schema that enables direct content extraction. If mention rate is high and citation rate is low, the content quality and authority signals are the bottleneck - not awareness.

## **Tier 3: Entity Authority
**

### **13. Entity consistency audit across all external profiles
**

What it is: A comparison of how the brand describes itself across all major external profiles to identify conflicting signals that create LLM uncertainty.

How to check: Pull the brand description from: Organization schema on homepage, LinkedIn company page About section, Crunchbase overview, G2 or Capterra profile, Wikipedia article if it exists, and the About page. Note any differences in: founding year, employee count range, core product description, target market, and geographic focus.

How to fix: Standardize descriptions across all profiles. Agree on the canonical one-paragraph brand description and update every profile to use it (or a close version). Ensure founding year, employee count, and other facts are consistent. Assign someone to audit external profiles quarterly.

### **14. Organization schema sameAs links to every official profile
**

What it is: The sameAs property in Organization schema linking the brand entity to all official external profiles, enabling LLMs to consolidate entity identity across sources.

How to check: Inspect Organization schema on the homepage (View Source or Rich Results Test). Does sameAs include: LinkedIn, Crunchbase, Wikipedia (if applicable), official social profiles, and industry-specific directories relevant to the category? Are all URLs live?

How to fix: Add all official profiles to the sameAs array. Verify every URL is live and points to the correct page. Review this when new official profiles are created. Remove any URLs that redirect or return errors.

### **15. Author Person schema connected to verifiable external profiles
**

What it is: Structured data connecting content to identifiable authors with verifiable credentials, implemented consistently across all content pages.

How to check: Click the author byline on a key content page. Does it link to a bio page? Does that bio page have Person schema with a sameAs array linking to LinkedIn and other published work? Is the same Person entity referenced consistently across all content from that author?

How to fix: Create bio pages for every content contributor with Person schema. Include sameAs links to LinkedIn at minimum, plus any academic profiles, industry publications, or other published work. Ensure the Article schema on each content page links to the correct Person entity rather than using a plain text author name.

### **16. dateModified in Article schema is actually updating when content is edited
**

What it is: A verification that the dateModified field in Article schema reflects the actual date of the last substantive content edit - not the original publication date or a static value set at launch.

How to check: Identify a piece of content that was recently edited. Use View Source or Rich Results Test to check the dateModified field. Does it match the actual edit date? Then check a piece of content that has not been edited in 12 months - is dateModified still stuck at the original publication date?

How to fix: Configure the CMS to automatically update dateModified whenever content is saved with substantive changes. Define what counts as a substantive change (updated statistics, new sections, revised conclusions - not typo fixes or formatting). Test the implementation after making a content edit to confirm the date updates.

### **17. Internal linking creates topical clusters around highest-priority AI Search pages
**

What it is: An assessment of whether the site's internal linking structure reinforces the authority of the pages most important for AI Search.

How to check: Identify the 5 pages most important for AI Search (the pages you most want cited). For each page: how many internal links point to it from relevant pages? Are those linking pages topically related? Or is the key page effectively an orphan?

How to fix: Build topical clusters around key pages. Every related piece of content on the site should link to the central authoritative page on that topic. Create a hub-and-spoke structure where the most important pages sit at the hub with multiple relevant spokes linking in. Use descriptive anchor text that reflects the topic of the destination page.

### **18. Heading structure mirrors how users actually ask questions
**

What it is: An audit of whether H2 and H3 headings on content pages use natural language that matches real user queries.

How to check: Extract all H2 and H3 headings from a key page. Search each heading as a query on Google. Does a similar phrase appear in People Also Ask? Does the heading read like a natural question or answer? Or like an internal section label?

How to fix: Rewrite headings using one of two patterns: question format matching real queries ("How do you measure AI search visibility?") or direct answer summaries that use the vocabulary of the query ("Measuring AI search visibility: the three metrics that matter"). Avoid: "Overview," "Background," "Introduction," "Key Considerations," and other internal labels that carry no query signal.

### **19. Content structure passes the inverted pyramid test
**

What it is: A verification that the most important information on each key page appears in the first paragraph, rather than building toward it through context-setting or background.

How to check: Show only the first paragraph of a key page to someone unfamiliar with the brand or topic. Ask them: what is this page about? What is the main answer or point? If they cannot answer confidently from the first paragraph alone, the page fails.

How to fix: Rewrite page openings using the inverted pyramid structure. The core answer goes first. Supporting evidence, context, and nuance follow. This applies to articles, guides, comparison pages, and product pages. The only exception is content structured as a narrative - but even then, an answer-first summary or key takeaway at the top improves AI Search performance.

### **20. Track brand position in LLM responses over time - not just presence
**

What it is: A monthly measurement of where in LLM responses the brand appears - first, alongside peers, or late - rather than simply whether it appears.

How to check: In monthly query testing, for every response where the brand appears, record its position: first brand named, named alongside others in the first sentence, named in a list, named late in the response after others, or named only in response to a direct brand query. Track this data in a spreadsheet month over month.

How to fix: Position in response is a lagging indicator of entity authority and content quality. Improving it requires the full stack of AI Search work - stronger entity signals, more cited content, named frameworks, and original data. But tracking it gives you a more sensitive signal than mention rate alone. Brands often see position improving before they see mention rate improving, which means position tracking gives you earlier evidence that the AI Search strategy is working.

## **What does it do?
**

Most AI search audits test whether your brand appears in search results. This prompt does something different: it asks the LLM to report on everything it knows and does not know about your brand directly from its training data. You get a complete picture of how AI systems currently understand, frame, and position your brand - associations, competitive framing, content source analysis, and a diagnosis of exactly which AI search problem you have.

## **How to use it?
**

Replace everything in [brackets] with your brand details

Paste the full prompt into Claude or ChatGPT as a new conversation

Run it once per brand - treat the output as your AI search baseline

Use the diagnosis in Section 6 to prioritize what to fix first

Prompt Template:

I want you to conduct a thorough brand intelligence audit on [BRAND NAME].
